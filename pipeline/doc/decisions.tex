\section*{Why this document exists}

The website reports a price wedge for an individual firm. The paper the site
is built on (Binsbergen, Boons, Opp and Tamoni, \emph{Dynamic Asset
(Mis)Pricing: Build-up versus Resolution Anomalies}) estimates price wedges
for decile portfolios and carries them to firms by a regression on
characteristics. Rebuilding that construction from raw CRSP and Compustat
data exposed choices inside it that the paper does not discuss and that move
the number a firm is given by ten to twenty percentage points. Some of those
choices were made one way in the paper and would be made another way if the
objective is the best possible firm-level estimate rather than a replication.

This document records each such choice: what the options are, what evidence
separates them, which was chosen for the site and why, and what would change
the decision. It is meant to be read on its own; the supporting numbers are
in \texttt{quality.pdf} (the mapping and level tests), \texttt{signals.pdf}
(every signal's construction) and the scripts named in the appendix. Every
number quoted here comes from a script that can be re-run.

The order follows the logic of the construction: what a wedge is, what a
firm's wedge should mean, which signals define the portfolios, and which
sample the wedges are estimated on.

\section{The object: what a price wedge is, and the one calibration that
defines it}

A price wedge is minus the log of the expected discounted cash flow, over the
fifteen years after formation, per dollar of price at formation:
\[
\mathrm{PW} \;=\; -\log \, \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{180} m_{t,t+s}\, D_{t+s} + m_{t,t+180}\, P_{t+180}}{P_t}\right],
\]
where $m$ is a stochastic discount factor of the CAPM form and the
expectation is the mean over formation cohorts (one cohort per month, July
1964 onward). A negative wedge means the price is below the discounted cash
flows that followed: underpricing. The discount factor has one free
parameter, the price of market risk $\lambda$, and the paper fixes it by one
condition: \textbf{the wedge of the value-weighted market portfolio is
zero}. Everything else is measured relative to that. A wedge is therefore a
statement about a group of dollars relative to the average dollar in the
market. This calibration is the identifying normalisation of the whole
method and is kept unchanged in every decision below.

Two properties of the object matter for what follows. First, a portfolio's
wedge is a dollar-weighted quantity: it is the wedge of one dollar spread
across the portfolio's members in proportion to their capitalisation, so it
is set by the large members. Second, a wedge is imprecise: the 463
formation cohorts of the paper's window overlap in 179 of their 180 months
and are worth two or three independent observations, so a single portfolio
wedge carries a standard error near 30 percentage points, almost all of it
common to every portfolio (the market's realised path), with about 3 to 4
points that is specific to the portfolio.

One realised number frames the rest of the document. With the paper's
$\lambda$, the value-weighted universe has a wedge of zero by construction.
The \emph{equal-weighted} universe, one dollar in every firm, has a realised
wedge of $-24.6$ percentage points. The average firm is a small firm, and
under this discount factor small firms are heavily underpriced. So ``the
average dollar'' and ``the average firm'' are 25 points apart, and any
firm-level number has to say which of the two it is about.

\section{Decision A: what the firm-level number means (taken: candidate 3)}

\subsection*{How a portfolio wedge is carried to a firm}

The 57 characteristics give 570 decile portfolios with wedges. Each
portfolio is described by a \emph{profile}: its members' percentile ranks on
all 57 characteristics, averaged over the members and over time. A
regression of the 570 wedges on the 570 profiles gives a rule from
characteristics to wedge, and evaluating that rule at a firm's own ranks
gives the firm's wedge. The paper does this with three principal components
of the profiles of the 114 extreme deciles; the pipeline does it with a
penalised regression on all 570, which is better on every portfolio-level
criterion (\texttt{quality.pdf}, first section). That choice is not the
decision here. The decision is what ``profile'' means, and it was hidden.

A profile can describe a portfolio's typical \emph{member}: the
equal-weighted mean of the members' ranks. Or it can describe its typical
\emph{dollar}: the capitalisation-weighted mean. For the value-weighted
book-to-market decile 10, the typical member has a size rank near 0.4 and
the typical dollar a size rank near 0.98, because a few large firms hold
most of the decile's capitalisation. The paper's code uses the equal-weighted
mean (with the comment ``SHOULD THIS BE VALUE WEIGHTED AVERAGE?????'' beside
it); the pipeline had used the capitalisation-weighted mean. This one choice
moves every firm-level wedge by ten to twenty-seven points, and it is the
whole reason the pipeline's firm wedges sat eleven points below the
published ones. With equal-weighted profiles the pipeline reproduces the
published firm wedges to 1.2 points in level (slope 1.04, correlation
0.99); the residual is the paper's log-bias correction on the portfolio
wedges, which is about one point and enters once, on the left-hand side.

\subsection*{The test that decides between candidates}

Correlation between two firm-level series says nothing about levels, and
levels are what the site prints. The test used throughout is the
\emph{realised-wedge test}: treat a candidate firm wedge as a
characteristic, sort firms into deciles on it every month at NYSE
breakpoints, hold each decile fifteen years as an equal-weighted and as a
value-weighted portfolio, compute the decile's realised wedge with the same
discount factor, and regress it on the mean wedge the candidate assigned to
the decile's members. A candidate whose levels can be trusted shows a slope
of one and an intercept of zero. Equal-weighted deciles answer ``do firms
realise what they were told'', which is the website's question;
value-weighted deciles answer ``does the candidate respect the calibration''.
A second check follows directly from the calibration: the
capitalisation-weighted mean of all firm wedges must equal the market wedge,
zero.

\subsection*{The three candidates}

\paragraph{Candidate 1: the published construction.} Value-weighted portfolio
wedges regressed on equal-weighted profiles. The regression learns ``a
portfolio whose typical member is a small value firm has wedge $W$'', but
$W$ was earned by the portfolio's large members. Evaluated at a small firm's
own ranks, the small firm receives the large firm's wedge. The test shows it:
on equal-weighted deciles the published series has slope 0.64 and intercept
$-18$ points; the firms it calls fairly priced (decile 5, assigned $-4$)
realise $-25$; only the most underpriced decile realises what it is assigned.
Its firm-level average is $-7$ against a realised $-22$ for the same firms,
and its capitalisation-weighted mean is $+9$ where the calibration requires
zero. The sentence the site could claim under this candidate is ``this is
the number the JFE method assigns'', which is true and is not what firms
like this one realised.

\paragraph{Candidate 2: per dollar.} Value-weighted wedges regressed on
value-weighted profiles. Now the regression is internally consistent: ``a
dollar in firms with size rank 0.98 and book-to-market rank 0.95 earned
wedge $W$'', and $W$ belongs to those dollars. The difficulty is where the
rule is evaluated. Nearly every portfolio's dollar-profile has a size rank
above 0.85 and the site asks about firms with size rank 0.4, so the fitted
line is extended far outside the points it was fitted on. The average is
right (capitalisation-weighted mean $+1$, intercepts $-3$) and the extremes
are exaggerated: the most underpriced decile is assigned about $-70$ and
realises $-42$; the slopes are 0.67 equal-weighted and 0.79
value-weighted. Heavier shrinkage repairs the value-weighted slope (1.02 at
a penalty of 1000) and not the equal-weighted one (0.79). The sentence: ``a
dollar invested in firms like this one was mispriced by $X$''. Right for
the market as a whole, exaggerated for small firms.

\paragraph{Candidate 3: per firm, with the aggregate pinned.} Add to the
regression the same 570 deciles held with \emph{equal} weights, whose wedge
is the wedge of the average member, each described by the equal-weighted
profile of its members, alongside the value-weighted deciles with their
value-weighted profiles. Both kinds of evidence are then in the fit: ``the
average small value firm realised $-42$'' from the equal-weighted decile,
next to ``the average value dollar realised $-32$'' from the value-weighted
one. A small firm's number is interpolated from portfolios whose members
resemble it, and the value-weighted portfolios keep the dollar-weighted total
anchored. On the test: slope 0.86 to 0.88 and intercept within one point on
equal-weighted deciles, mean absolute gap of 3.5 to 4.5 points across the
ten deciles, capitalisation-weighted mean of all firm wedges 0.0 points. Out
of sample (fitted on cohorts formed before 1988, evaluated on the later
cohorts) the slopes hold at 0.87 to 0.99 and the level moves with the
market, as every candidate's does. The sentence: ``firms with this profile
were mispriced by $X$ on average, each firm counting once; dollar-weighted
across all firms, mispricing is zero, as the calibration requires.''

\subsection*{The decision and its reasons}

Candidate 3 is adopted for the site's headline series. The reasons, in
order of weight:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item It is the only candidate whose number was checked against realised
outcomes at the level of the firm and found right, equal-weighted and
value-weighted at once.
\item Its sentence is the one a user looking up a single company expects the
number to mean.
\item It keeps the paper's calibration intact rather than working around it:
the value-weighted portfolios remain in the fit and the aggregate check
holds at 0.0.
\item It does not sacrifice ranking: all three candidates rank firms
similarly (correlations 0.9 and above); the candidates differ in levels, and
levels are the objective.
\end{enumerate}
The published construction (candidate 1) is kept as a labelled reference
series, because it is what the paper reports and readers will want to see
the relation. Candidate 2 is not shown.

\subsection*{What this changes in the construction}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Equal-weighted decile portfolios are formed for every sort
(\texttt{portfolio\_wedges.py -{}-weighting equal}) and priced at the
$\lambda$ of the value-weighted calibration; the calibration itself is not
touched.
\item Profiles are computed with the weighting of the portfolio they
describe (\texttt{portfolio\_profiles.py -{}-weighting equal} for the
equal-weighted set).
\item The mapping is a penalised direct regression on the 1,140 portfolios
with their matching profiles. The penalty is chosen by the
leave-one-family-out fit and accepted only if the realised-level test passes
(slope of at least 0.85 and intercept within about 3 points equal-weighted;
capitalisation-weighted mean within a point or two of the market wedge).
\item The realised-level test and the aggregate check become acceptance
gates in the pipeline (\texttt{realised\_levels.py}); no series goes to the
site without them.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{What would change the decision}

Evidence that the equal-weighted portfolios' wedges are too noisy to help
(they are not: their idiosyncratic standard errors are of the same size as
the value-weighted ones), or a decision that the site's number should be a
per-dollar statement. Neither is expected.

\subsection*{A consequence for the paper}

The published firm-level wedges are candidate 1. Any use of them in levels
inherits the $-18$-point intercept: the average firm's mispricing is
understated by about that amount, and firm-level regressions that use the
wedge as a regressor in levels (the investment-$q$ analysis) are affected.
Rankings are not. This is worth raising with the coauthors before the
extension to more characteristics is built.

\section{Decision B: which signal definitions the site's portfolios are
built on (open; default: as described)}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

The mapping learns from the wedges of the 570 (now 1,140) portfolios, and a
portfolio is defined by how its sorting characteristic was built. The
rebuild verified 56 of the 57 characteristics against the paper's own panels,
and in doing so found six places where what the paper did is not what the
paper describes. For the site the question is not which is right in the
replication sense (both are known now) but which the site should claim as
the definition of its inputs.

\subsection*{The sources, signal by signal}

The rule adopted for this decision is that each signal is defined by the
paper the anomaly comes from, read through Freyberger, Neuhierl and Weber
(2020), whose appendix is the list the paper takes its characteristics from
and the source of Table~A.1's wording. What was checked, from the original
texts where they could be obtained (15 September 2026):

\paragraph{Operating accruals (OA) and their absolute value (AOA).}
Sloan (1996) defines accruals as the change in non-cash current assets minus
the change in current liabilities other than debt in current liabilities and
taxes payable, \emph{minus} depreciation, scaled by average total assets (the
Chen--Zimmermann rendering of Sloan's equation). FNW state the same
components, scaled by lagged total assets. The paper's data adds depreciation
instead of subtracting it; the sort it produces agrees with the documented
one at only 0.29. AOA is the absolute value of OA (Bandyopadhyay, Huang and
Wirjanto 2010). \textbf{Settled: subtract depreciation, scale by lagged total
assets as FNW do.} The scaling difference between Sloan and FNW is immaterial
for a rank.

\paragraph{Standardised unexplained volume (SUV).} Garfinkel (2009,
equations 4--6) defines a \emph{daily} measure: $\mathrm{SUV}_{i,t} =
\mathrm{UV}_{i,t}/S_{i}$, where $\mathrm{UV}_{i,t}$ is the day's volume
minus $\hat\alpha_i + \hat\beta_1 |R_{i,t}|^{+} + \hat\beta_2 |R_{i,t}|^{-}$,
the regression estimated over a control window that precedes the measurement
day, and $S_i$ the standard deviation of that regression's residuals over the
estimation window. In his event setting the measure is averaged over the
event days. FNW turn it into a monthly characteristic with the regression
estimated on the previous month and the unexplained volume taken in the
current month, which is what Table~A.1 says. Neither source singles out one
day. The paper's data is the last trading day's residual from a regression
run within the same month. \textbf{Settled: for every trading day of month
$t$, the standardised residual from the regression estimated on the days of
month $t-1$; the characteristic is the mean over the days of month $t$.} The
aggregation by the mean is the one choice not dictated by the sources; it is
Garfinkel's own aggregation over his event window.

\paragraph{Detrended turnover (DTO).} Garfinkel (2009, equation 3) defines
daily market-adjusted turnover as the firm's volume over its shares minus
the same ratio for all NYSE and AMEX stocks, and ``change in turnover'' as
that quantity minus its \emph{mean} over a control period (twenty trading
days). FNW's characteristic subtracts the daily market turnover and detrends
by the firm's own 180-trading-day median, scaling Nasdaq volume down by 38\%
after 1997 and by 50\% before, following Anderson and Dyl (2005), who
document that dealer participation roughly doubles reported Nasdaq volume
(a 38\% reduction in their 1997--2002 sample; practitioners use about 50\%
before). The paper's data takes the last day's raw turnover minus its own
180-day mean, with no market adjustment and no scaling. \textbf{Settled:
FNW's definition, averaged over the days of the month: daily
market-adjusted turnover minus its trailing 180-trading-day median, Nasdaq
volume scaled as in Anderson and Dyl.} Garfinkel's own detrending window is
an event-study control period and has no monthly analogue; FNW's 180-day
median is the anomaly-literature version and the one the paper cites.

\paragraph{Turnover (TNOVR).} Datar, Naik and Radcliffe (1998) measure
turnover as the average number of shares traded over the preceding three
months divided by shares outstanding as of the period, excluding stocks
whose share count changed in those months. FNW's characteristic is
``last month's volume over shares outstanding'': the one-month version,
with the volume lagged relative to the share count exactly as in Datar, Naik
and Radcliffe. The paper's data is last month's volume over this month's
shares, which \emph{is} FNW's definition; Table~A.1 dropped the words ``last
month's''. \textbf{Settled: no change.} The earlier classification of this
signal as a documentation discrepancy is withdrawn; it was a misreading of
the condensed appendix.

\paragraph{Industry-adjusted book-to-market, size, profit margin and asset
turnover (aBEME, aSIZE, aPM, aSAT).} FNW: the characteristic minus its
average at the Fama-French 48 industry level, citing Asness, Porter and
Stevens (2000) for book-to-market and size and Soliman (2008) for profit
margin and asset turnover. The paper's data demeans within 27 industry
groups of unknown origin, recovered from its panels, with the CRSP
name-history SIC code. \textbf{Open: awaiting the original texts.} Both are
behind access walls (SSRN and The Accounting Review). What to look for in
them: the industry classification (SIC digits or Fama-French industries and
how many), whether the benchmark is the industry mean or median, whether it
is equal- or value-weighted, and any exclusions. Until then the default is
FNW's rendering, the Fama-French 48 mean. Note that FNW's own ``ATO'' is
Soliman's asset turnover (sales over lagged net operating assets) while
``SAT'' is sales over total assets; the paper's aSAT adjusts the latter.

\paragraph{aPM.} Same construction; in addition its cell means cannot be
rebuilt (they were computed over a wider firm set than the panels hold) and
its long-short alpha is 0.00012 a month, so the long leg is not identified.
\textbf{Settled: dropped from the site's portfolios; the aPM sort is not a
signal in any meaningful sense.}

\paragraph{Conventions kept.} The panels revealed several implementation
choices that the sources leave open and that do not change what a signal
measures: the beta window (the firm's last 249 trading days), zero-volume
days excluded from the volume dispersion, $n-1$ in the residual standard
deviation, the day-level range-or-quote spread, dividends in dollars over
market capitalisation. These are documented in \texttt{signals.pdf} and kept.

\subsection*{The options}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{As the paper did it.} The site's portfolios are the paper's.
Table~1 reproduces row by row and the reference series and the headline
series share one set of portfolios. Six portfolios do not mean what their
names say: the accruals sort is partly a depreciation sort; the SUV and DTO
sorts are one-day signals sampled on the last trading day.
\item \textbf{As the paper describes it.} The six are rebuilt from the
documented definitions and aPM is dropped. Portfolios mean what they say
and the site can cite the definitions. Six rows of the portfolio table
differ from the JFE numbers and this has to be stated; the mapping loses one
regressor.
\item \textbf{Signal by signal.} For instance the accruals and industry fixes
with SUV and DTO kept as published, on the argument that those two carry
small wedges either way ($-5$ and $-6$ points on the long leg).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{What the evidence says about the size of the choice}

The firm-level series is affected modestly: six of 57 regressors and six of
570 portfolios per weighting. The portfolio-level table is affected exactly
in those six rows. The conventions the panels revealed that do \emph{not}
change what a signal measures are kept under every option: the 249-trading-day
window for the daily beta, zero-volume days excluded from the volume
dispersion, $n-1$ in the idiosyncratic volatility, the day-level spread,
dividends in dollars over market capitalisation.

\subsection*{Default, pending the decision}

Option 2, with option 1 run alongside as the labelled reference. The reason
is the same as in decision A: the site should be able to state what its
inputs are in one sentence, and ``as defined in the paper's appendix'' is a
sentence, while ``as in the paper's data, including a depreciation term with
the wrong sign and two signals sampled on one day'' is not one the site
should have to make. The cost, six rows that differ from the published
table, is disclosed rather than hidden.

\section{Decision C: which cohorts the wedges are estimated on (open;
default: the full available sample)}

\subsection*{The options}

The paper's window (formations July 1964 to December 2002, cash flows to
2017; 463 cohorts), the full available sample (formations to January 2011,
cash flows to December 2025; 559 cohorts), or a later window.

\subsection*{What the evidence says}

\texttt{stability.py} re-estimates all 570 wedges on subsamples of the
cohorts, re-solving $\lambda$ on each so the market wedge is zero there.
Extending the paper's window to the full sample changes almost nothing:
correlation 0.99 between the two cross-sections, one long-short sign change,
$\lambda$ from 3.97 to 3.53 on the same data. Dropping the early cohorts is
different. The two halves (1964--87 and 1988--2011) correlate 0.45 where two
halves of a constant cross-section would correlate about 0.68 given their
noise; the value ratios reverse sign between halves, profitability spreads
shrink, momentum grows; the 1980--95 third is nearly flat and unrelated to
the rest. Any window starting in 1980 or later correlates 0.5 to 0.8 with
the full cross-section, rests on one or two fifteen-year cycles ($\lambda$
swings from 5.6 to 2.5 to 2.8 across the thirds), and moves the typical
firm's wedge by 10 to 20 points. The firm ranking is robust to the choice
(correlations 0.9 and above); the level is not.

\subsection*{Default, pending the decision}

The full available sample as the headline, with the two half-sample
estimates shown as a range next to each firm's number. A later window is not
a better estimate of the same quantity; it is a different regime, and the
site should show that regime dependence rather than pick a side.

\section{What the site shows, given A and the defaults for B and C}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Headline:} the candidate-3 firm wedge, full sample, documented
signal definitions, with its half-sample range and an extrapolation flag for
firms whose characteristics lie outside the profiles' support.
\item \textbf{Reference:} the published construction (candidate 1, paper's
definitions, paper's window), labelled ``as published in the JFE''.
\item \textbf{Method page:} the definition of the wedge, the calibration
(value-weighted market wedge zero), the weighting choices and the sentence
each number can claim, the acceptance tests and their latest values.
\item \textbf{Uncertainty:} the common component of the standard error
(about 30 points, the market's realised path, the same for every firm) shown
once as the uncertainty of the whole level; the firm-specific part (about 3
to 4 points relative, and the half-sample range of about $\pm12$) shown per
firm.
\end{itemize}

\section{Acceptance gates for every build}

A series is published only if all of these hold on the latest run:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item Replication: the ``as published'' spec verifies at least 56 of 57
characteristics against the paper's panels and Table~1 within a point on
both legs.
\item Level test: slope of at least 0.85 and intercept within 3 points on
equal-weighted deciles; slope of at least 0.75 on value-weighted deciles;
the decile table reported.
\item Aggregate check: capitalisation-weighted mean of firm wedges within a
point or two of the market wedge.
\item Out-of-sample fit: leave-one-family-out strict $R^2$ of the mapping
reported and not below the previous build's by more than noise.
\item Ex-post returns: the fitted-wedge deciles' subsequent returns monotone
at five years.
\end{enumerate}
